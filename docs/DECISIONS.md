# Decisions

Choices made where the spec was open or didn't work as written, and why. Over the Cape started from the code of [4WD Simulator](https://github.com/reidstephen11/4wd-simulator) v0.18; its decisions are kept below, under "Inherited from 4WD Simulator", because they still describe the engine (its maps now live on only as test worlds in `tests/fixtures/maps`). Over the Cape's own decisions follow, one section per version.

# Inherited from 4WD Simulator

### Build and tooling

- **Vitest 3.2 instead of the newest major.** npm 10's resolver crashed (`Cannot read properties of null (reading 'edgesOut')`) on the optional peer chain of Vitest 4/5. Vitest 3.2.7 installs cleanly and does everything the spec needs.
- **TypeScript 5.9.** TypeScript 6+/7 is outside the range `typescript-eslint` supports, so lint would break.
- **Playwright 1.56.1.** This is the version whose Chromium build (1194) is preinstalled at `/opt/pw-browsers`, so nothing is downloaded.
- **Vite `base: './'`.** Relative asset paths work under the GitHub Pages project URL (`/4wd-simulator/`) and on any other static host or sub-folder, which §2 also asks for.
- **Shadows: `PCFShadowMap` with `shadow.radius`.** §4 asks for "PCF soft". In three r186 `PCFSoftShadowMap` was removed (it logs a warning and falls back); plain PCF now does soft filtering with a sampling radius, so that is used.
- **Sky is not tone-mapped.** The sky dome shows the Environment colours exactly as authored (§4 horizon/zenith hex values). Lit surfaces go through ACES Filmic with exposure 0.56 (three's ACES multiplies by 1/0.6 internally, so 1.0 washed the palette out).

### World

- **Map is 1536 m × 1536 m** (spec: ~1.5 km) so it divides into 64 m chunks and a 1 m heightfield (1537 × 1537 samples). The playable area is ±716 m; border mountains and an invisible wall close it off.
- **Coordinates:** x = east, z = south (north is −z), y = up, origin at the map centre. Headings are compass degrees (0 = north, 90 = east).
- **Escarpments, hills and ridges as authoring primitives.** `data/map.ts` describes the land with a few shapes (a north escarpment up to the high plain, a low east bench the creek drops off at the Falls, hills, a ridge crest) plus the creek and track splines. Base noise adds rolling relief; detail noise adds 1 m bumps.
- **The Falls are a ~11 m drop off a low bench in the east**, not off the high plain. A 60 m waterfall off the plain wouldn't be "small", and the creek has to run east–west through the middle of the map.
- **Creek water level** is a per-point water surface along the creek spline plus one global `Environment.waterLevelOffset` (§11's "single parameter").
- **All named features are discoverable POIs**, not only the landmarks: Gravel Ford, Deep Crossing, Bog Hole, Washout Gully, Fallen Tree and The Staircase get discovery radii and appear on the map once found, alongside the campsites, viewpoints and landmarks. The Main track and The Creek are linear features, so they are drawn on the map but not "discovered".
- **Terrain rendering** is a fixed-depth quadtree (256 m blocks at 8 m resolution far away down to 64 m chunks at 1 m near the camera) instead of uniform 64 m chunks. Uniform chunks meant 300+ draw calls; the quadtree keeps it under ~100. LOD 0 uses exactly the physics triangles. Skirts hide cracks between levels.
- **Distance haze** uses a custom fog patch: warm `#E8CFA6` close in, shifting to `#9FB4C4` on far ridges, capped below 100% so far ridges stay visible as blue silhouettes. A ring of low-poly backdrop ridges sits outside the map so the horizon is never empty.
- **Terrain does not cast shadows.** With the sun at 25° the terrain's long self-shadows were cut off in a hard line at the edge of the shadow area that follows the car. Flat-shaded faces already show the relief; trees, rocks, props and the vehicle still cast shadows.

### Vehicle physics (M2)

Rapier's `DynamicRayCastVehicleController` is the base, as §2 asks. A few of its built-in behaviours didn't fit the §6.2 feel targets, so the vehicle wraps it:

- **Rapier's terrain triangulation.** Rapier splits each heightfield cell along the (x+1, z)–(x, z+1) diagonal and stores heights x-major. `Terrain.heightAt`, the render mesh (LOD 0) and the collider all use that split; a test checks they agree to within 1 cm.
- **Suspension rest length = full droop.** The controller's ray only reaches `restLength + radius`, so wheels could never drop below the rest length. The rest length is set to the fully extended length; the car sits 0.12 m below that on its springs, leaving 0.13 m of bump travel (0.25 m total, §6.1). Wheels drop into holes.
- **Bump-stop spheres** sit where each wheel is at full compression, so the car can't drive "through" walls, ledges or logs with its ray-only wheels.
- **Missing roll torque added back.** The controller applies tyre side forces near the centre-of-mass height (a fixed 10% "roll influence" that isn't exposed in the JS API), so the car could never roll. The vehicle adds the missing torque each step (`rollTorqueScale` in `data/vehicle.ts`, 1.0 = physical).
- **Traction and open diffs are computed by the vehicle, not the controller.** Each wheel's drive force is capped at grip × load. Each axle behaves like an open diff — it can only push as hard as its weaker wheel allows, plus a 35% "leak" (`openDiffLeak`). With a textbook open diff (0%), a single lifted wheel stops the car dead, which felt punishing on the Staircase; 35% keeps line choice important without making it a coin toss.
- **Anti-roll bars** shift spring preload between left and right wheels of an axle (only while both touch the ground), so they move load through the tyres like a real bar instead of adding a torque from nowhere.
- **Gears.** A 3-speed automatic with a 2.5:1 low range (§6.1: ~2.5× wheel torque). Engine torque × gearing gives about 5.8 kN at the wheels in high range, which is why high range struggles past ~20° and low range climbs 30°. Top speeds (100 / 25 km/h) are enforced by a soft governor per range, because gearing alone would let the car reach ~160 km/h in high and ~60 km/h in low.
- **Driving model.** S brakes when moving forwards and selects reverse once stopped (like most driving games); W does the same in reverse. A hold brake keeps the car still when stopped with no input, except while winching. Range changes are allowed below 20 km/h.
- **Measured against §6.2** (headless tests in `tests/vehicle.test.ts`): 5 s full throttle on flat dirt ≈ 40 m; low range climbs a 30° ramp from a standstill, high range slides back; high range still creeps up 18°; a hard turn at 50 km/h on a 20° side slope rolls the car; the same turn on flat ground at 40 km/h slides but stays upright; it drives across a 31° slope and rolls across 38°. The roll on a 20° slope happens when turning _down_ the slope (gravity keeps the speed up); turning hard _up_ a 20° slope at 50 km/h lifts the inside wheels but the car slows enough to survive.
- **Debug free camera** toggles with the backtick key (debug mode only) or the button in the debug panel. C selects crawler views.

### World content (M3)

- **Static scatter collides as a few triangle meshes.** Rapier's step time grows with the number of separate colliders (~1 ms per step for 8,000 tree trunks). Trunks, rocks, logs and props are merged into one trimesh per 256 m cell and surface type, which brings the step back to ~0.1 ms. The Staircase ledges and washout walls stay as exact boxes.
- **Every tree inside the playable area has a trunk collider and can be a winch anchor.** The graphics "vegetation density" setting only thins out grass tussocks and shrubs (which don't collide), so invisible trees never block the car on Low.
- **Trees:** four eucalypt types — tall mountain ash in the valley, mid-size candlebark on the slopes, short twisted snow gums on the high plain, and a few dead stags — each with two shapes and a far-away low-detail version. They're drawn with `BatchedMesh`, which culls per tree and switches detail per tree.
- **Marked anchor boulders** carry a pale paint ring and a steel ring bolt so they read as anchors. About a third of the larger scattered boulders are marked, plus hand-placed ones by the Bog Hole and on the Staircase.
- **Washout Gully:** a 3 m trench in the heightfield plus vertical earth walls (static boxes) that leave exactly a 1.2 m gap where the track crosses; the 1 m heightfield can't represent a 1.2 m gap on its own. The gully runs 112 m across the slope so the detour around its top end is the only way past without planks.
- **The Staircase** is a 25–30° rocky climb with granite ledges (0.3 m risers every 5.5 m) as static boxes across the track, and a switchback with some camber.
- **The Fallen Tree** is a 750 kg dynamic log with branch stubs (so it doesn't roll like a pipe), lying across the main track in a small clearing so it can also be driven around slowly.
- **Track ruts** are thin ribbons drawn on top of the terrain; faint tracks are only these two wheel lines over the grass.
- **"Reachable by driving"** is checked headlessly: for each POI the car starts 50 m back along its approach track and an autopilot drives it into the discovery radius (`tests/poi.test.ts`). The M3 screenshots park the car on that approach.
- **Props face their heading** (the front of a hut, the mine entrance, a sign face). Sign lettering is drawn into small canvas textures with the UI font.

### Tools and light damage (M4)

- **Winch** is a spring-damper cable from the front bumper to the anchor (60 kN/m, capped at 1.5× vehicle weight). The motor can't reel in against a full-load pull (it stalls) but can always pay out. The hold brake releases while the winch is attached and pulling, so the car rolls with it. Quick attach scores anchors by distance with a penalty for ones behind the car. Anchors: every tree trunk (strap at ~1 m), marked boulders (ring bolt), the Fallen Tree (hooked at the point on the trunk nearest the winch) and — for future co-op — other vehicles' rear tow point.
- **Wheels push back on what they stand on.** Rapier's ray-cast wheels don't apply forces to dynamic ground, so a plank never "felt" the car and got shoved aside. The vehicle now applies each wheel's load and tyre forces to dynamic bodies under it (planks, the Fallen Tree).
- **Planks** are 45 kg dynamic boards with high friction. The collider is 0.42 m wide (drawn 0.3 m) so a ray-cast wheel — which is a single line, not a tyre — doesn't drop off the edge when it's a few centimetres off. The preview rests each plank on whatever is under its ends (ray cast against terrain, rocks and the gully walls). While placing planks or picking an anchor the car sits on its handbrake and the camera rises to look at the ground ahead.
- **Approach and departure angles.** The body collider is split into a low section between the axles (0.28 m clearance) and raised front and rear overhangs (~37° approach, ~33° departure), like a short-wheelbase 4WD. One full-length box gave only ~21° and the bumper caught on plank ends and ledges. Bumpers and the winch were raised on the model to match.
- **Washout Gully walls follow the ground.** Each wall's top is tilted to match the slope across the gully, so there's no artificial step at the gap and planks lie nearly flat.
- **Engine braking** was doubled: in low range it now holds the car on moderate descents, which is what low range is for (and stops the car creeping into planks while you place them).
- **Jack:** after 2 s tipped past 60° and nearly still, holding J for 1.5 s switches the body to kinematic for a 1.2 s animation (lift, rotate upright keeping the heading, set down ~1 m towards where the roof pointed), then back to dynamic.
- **Engine flooding** follows §6.4 exactly; the sputter (random power cuts from 5 s) uses a per-vehicle seeded random generator so the simulation stays deterministic.
- **Dirt** builds per zone (front, sides, rear, wheels) from the surface's dirt rate times speed — or wheelspin, so a car bogged in mud gets muddy — and water washes it down to 25% of its level (wheels fully). **Dents** come from a sudden velocity change above 3.5 m/s; the hit side is found from the direction of that change, and nearby body vertices are pushed in (max 11 cm, 28 dents kept).
- **Bogged wheelspin:** with the throttle in and the car barely moving on loose ground, the wheels show spin (visual, dirt and sound only) even though the physics model treats mud as extra rolling resistance rather than lost traction.
- **Headless M4 checks** (`tests/tools.test.ts`): 11 s with the intake under water stalls the engine (and it restarts only after 20 s clear); winching in closes the distance to the anchor; the Bog Hole holds the car in high range and the winch pulls it out; the Washout Gully stops the car without planks and it crosses on planks (driven along the planks at a crawl, as a player would steer).

### Exploration UI and saving (M5)

- **Every named feature is a discoverable POI** (14 in total, including both campsites). The campsite you spawn at is discovered straight away, because the car starts inside it.
- **Saving** writes one `localStorage` key holding discovered POIs, the last campsite visited and the settings. It saves when something is discovered, when a campsite is reached and when settings change. If storage is blocked or full the game keeps running with default settings and prints one console warning; nothing is shown on screen.
- **Pausing:** the paper map and the pause menu stop the simulation. The tool menu (hold Q) doesn't pause, but it parks the car (no throttle, steering or brake input) while open, so you can choose a tool without the car wandering off.
- **Context prompts** only show what can be done right now: "Attach winch" appears below about 5 km/h with an anchor in reach, "Pick up planks" next to placed planks, "Hold J — Right vehicle" once the jack is available, and an engine warning while the intake is under water.
- **The compass strip** shows only discovered POIs within 400 m, so undiscovered places stay a surprise.
- **The paper map** is drawn once in the background after loading (relief shading, contour lines, creeks, tracks and forest), split into small chunks so it never freezes the game. Only discovered POIs are labelled. If it's opened before that finishes, it shows blank paper and fills in when ready.
- **Menus count every key press.** On a slow frame several presses can arrive together; menus apply each one instead of dropping all but the first.
- **Return to last campsite** fades to black, places the car at the campsite's parking spot facing its exit, and clears flooding (not dirt or dents).

### Audio (M6)

- **Everything is synthesised at start-up.** A few seconds of noise (white, deep "brown" noise, sparse grains for dirt, dense clicks for gravel, slow wet swells for mud) are generated once from a fixed seed and looped. No audio files.
- **The audio context is created on the first click, tap or key press** (not Escape, which browsers don't count as a gesture). Before that the game is silent and nothing is logged. If the browser has no Web Audio the game runs silently with one console warning. Audio pauses while the tab is hidden.
- **Engine:** a harmonic-rich tone at the firing frequency (rpm ÷ 60 × 2 for a four-cylinder), a rumble at half that, and combustion noise pulsed at the firing rate. Load mixes in distortion and noise and opens the filter, so it gets louder and grittier when working. When flooding cuts the power the sound drops out with it (the vehicle now exposes the power multiplier it applied). Stalling lets the pitch fall with the rpm, then a clunk; a restart plays a starter-motor crank before the engine catches.
- **Horn:** two-tone (H / L3). It's on the controls list, so it needed a sound.
- **Tyres:** one noise layer per surface (dirt, gravel, grass, rock, mud, water, wood planks) plus a "scrub" layer for sideways sliding. Each wheel on the ground adds a quarter of its surface's layer, scaled by speed and wheelspin, so crossing from dirt to gravel cross-fades. Deep water at a wheel switches it to the water layer, and dropping into water at speed splashes.
- **Suspension thumps** come from a new `wheelImpact` sim event: a wheel's suspension compressing faster than 1.2 m/s (`wheelImpactSpeed` in `data/vehicle.ts`). Doing this in the simulation keeps it independent of the frame rate. Cruising on flat ground makes none; rough tracks give roughly one a second. Dents also play a body crunch.
- **Winch:** motor whine whose pitch drops from 560 Hz towards 290 Hz as the pull nears the winch's limit (paying out is lighter), plus random cable creaks that come more often the harder it pulls.
- **Ambience:** wind rises from the valley floor (25 m) to the high plain (130 m) with slow random gusts and a faint whistle up high; creek water within 45 m of the water's edge (read from the precomputed creek-distance grid); a deeper roar within 140 m of The Falls; and bird calls every 9–32 s — a kookaburra-style laugh (low chuckles, a run of alternating "ha-ha" notes, a falling tail) or a magpie-style warble (sliding fluty notes with a fast flutter). Birds are quieter up high. The debug panel has buttons to play each call.
- **UI:** a paper rustle when the map opens (softer when it closes) and a rising four-note chime on discovery.
- **Volumes:** master, engine and ambience sliders map to gain as slider², which sounds more even across the range. Tyres, winch, thumps and horn sit under master only. While paused, vehicle sounds fade out and ambience drops to 60%. A gentle compressor on the master keeps peaks below clipping.
- **Checks** (`e2e/m6-audio.spec.ts`): the context is off until the first key press, then running; engine pitch rises when driving; tyre layers follow the surface; creek water is heard near the creek; master at 0 silences everything; engine at 0 mutes only the engine; a bird call peaks below 1.0; the winch whine plays when reeling in; no audio warnings or console errors; and with Web Audio removed the game still runs.

### Polish and performance (M7)

- **Particles** are three pooled instanced meshes (one draw call each): pale low-poly dust puffs that fade out (dry dirt, less on gravel and grass, mostly from the rear wheels, above 2.5 m/s), mud chunks thrown back off spinning tyres in mud, and water droplets plus expanding foam rings (a wake) in the creek. Dust and wake rings use a per-instance opacity; dust has a little self-lighting so the facets read as a soft puff rather than glass. Rates live in `data/particles.ts`; the quality preset scales them (Low 0.45×, Medium 1×, High 1.35×). No dust when the ground is wet (`Environment.wetness`), ready for weather later.
- **Distant trees** use a third, very simple model (a three-sided trunk and an eight-sided canopy, ~14 triangles instead of ~60) beyond `treeFarDistance` (Low 380 m, Medium 520 m, High 800 m). From a high lookout the forest still reads as forest, and the worst view (from the south edge across the whole map) dropped from ~650k to ~480k triangles at Medium.
- **Terrain chunk cache fix.** Chunks are cached and the least recently used are dropped. Only drawn chunks counted as "used", so on long drives the parents of the detailed chunks around the car were dropped and rebuilt every frame and the car drove on the coarse 8 m terrain. Every chunk the quadtree passes through now counts as used.
- **Camera polish.** When a tree or rock blocks the view the camera still moves in straight away, but now eases back out over about half a second once the view clears instead of popping. The look-at point follows the car's height with a little lag so the view doesn't shake on rough tracks (never more than 0.6 m behind).
- **Measuring.** The debug overlay now shows main-thread time per frame (simulation and rendering separately) and particle counts; the test hook can switch quality, report per-group draw calls and triangles, and run an autopilot along any track (used for the scripted drive).
- **What could be measured here.** This container has no GPU: Chromium renders with SwiftShader (software), which manages 4–7 fps at 1280×720 whatever the settings, so the "60 fps at Medium" check can't be confirmed here and needs a real laptop (open `?debug`). What the tests do measure, at Medium: 26–124 draw calls and 85k–483k triangles per frame over a 7 × 7 grid covering the whole map (median 76 calls, 269k triangles), including the shadow pass; physics ~0.1 ms per step in Node. Low, Medium and High at the same view: 79 / 91 / 97 calls and 253k / 330k / 450k triangles.
- **Scripted drive** (`e2e/m7-polish.spec.ts`): five minutes of autopilot driving along five long tracks in different parts of the map, sampling the stats every 3 s, with audio running. No console errors; draw calls and triangles stay within the grid's range. Because the software renderer is so slow, the simulation only gets through ~40 s in those five minutes (at most 5 physics steps per frame), so the distance check is against simulated time (the car averages over 3 m/s including starts).

### Graphics refinement (Over The Hill reference)

- The follow-up graphics request supersedes the strict flat-shaded-only rule in SPEC §4. The reference remains **Over The Hill**, with its clean silhouettes, warm light and atmospheric landscapes; the Australian map and unbranded vehicle remain original.
- Terrain retains the exact physics heights and triangle diagonal. Shared central-difference normals and narrow biome-colour blends remove the triangular patchwork. World-space procedural grain and small normal variations add surface detail; rock and mud weights control highlights and roughness. Detail fades with pixel footprint to limit distant shimmer.
- Tree canopies use generated eucalyptus branch cutouts with mipmaps and matching shadow cutouts. The matte foliage uses diffuse lighting to keep the dense canopy cheaper to render. Trees still use the same collider positions and three batched detail levels. Tussocks have finer, more numerous blades, and ground-cover density is higher. A single instanced meadow draw fills the gaps close to the camera with short, wind-swaying blades, tapering down at its boundary.
- Paint, glass, rubber and steel use separate materials. An indexed material grouping preserves the vertex arrays that dirt and dents modify. Wheels and the spare have rounder profiles, tread blocks, rims and hardware; doors and the grille have additional trim.
- A small filtered procedural sky supplies reflections to vehicle materials and animated creek water. Clear afternoon light, restrained cloud wisps, warm foreground haze and cooler distant ridges supply depth. No external art assets or dependencies were added.
- Low renders at native resolution rather than 0.8×. Medium and High allow up to 1.5× and 2× device pixel ratio respectively; High's shadow coverage is tighter for sharper nearby shadows. Resolution remains capped by the actual display density.
- Regression checks cover continuous normals/colours across terrain chunk and LOD boundaries, exact rendered-surface agreement with physics, and dirt/dent behavior after material grouping. Software-rendered browser captures verify the shaders and quality changes. A sustained browser driving smoke check timed out in SwiftShader; the headless driving and physics tests pass. Hardware frame rate still requires a real GPU.

### Detailed 40 Series-inspired vehicle

- The user requested substantially more vehicle detail based on an old Land Cruiser or Land Rover. The chosen reference is the 1970s Land Cruiser 40 Series. This replaces the earlier generic-only body direction; all geometry is modeled in code with no downloaded vehicle assets or copied logos. Reference proportions and details were checked against [restored FJ40 photography](https://www.fj.co/ourwork).
- The body is rebuilt from separate panels, with an arched opening at each wheel instead of a solid tub through the tyres. The bonnet is narrower than the front wings. The hardtop has rounded edges, rain gutters, framed glazing, opening-light dividers and split rear doors. Door seams, hinges, locks, mirror brackets and bonnet catches are modeled.
- The translucent windows reveal a right-hand-drive cabin with seats and seat stitching, dashboard instruments, steering spokes, gear and range levers, interior door pulls and rear benches. Cabin floors and wheel openings are checked for tyre clearance.
- The vehicle has rounded tyre carcasses, three staggered tread rows, sidewall rings, recessed steel rims, ventilation holes, six studs, locking hubs and valves. The same full-size wheel geometry is used for the rear spare, preserving its materials and smooth normals.
- The recovery equipment includes a cable-wound winch drum, motor, clutch housing, roller fairlead at the existing winch mount, brush bar, recovery shackles, spare carrier, pressed-steel jerry can, roof basket, strapped luggage, toolbox and two separately visible recovery planks. The aerial is cosmetic; the intake position and flooding behavior are unchanged.
- Solid axles tilt with the left/right wheel heights; dampers telescope between the chassis mounts and wheel positions. Leaf springs, chassis rails, crossmembers, transmission housing and exhaust are visible underneath. Brake-lamp material brightens from the simulated brake input.
- Eight shared material slots separate paint, metal, glass, rubber, white/red/amber lenses and fabric. Material grouping preserves vertex order for dirt and dents; untouched curved surfaces retain their original smooth normals after damage. The complete model uses approximately 62k triangles, including all four wheels, the spare, cabin, equipment and running gear.
- Validation includes model captures from front, rear, side and cabin views, an articulation pose and a muddy version, plus regression tests for wheel/floor clearance, axle movement and individual plank visibility. Driving tuning, colliders, saved progress and tool mount positions are unchanged.

### Rock crawling contact and clearance

- The detailed model's wheel openings and narrow chassis were still using the earlier solid collision boxes. Collision now follows the frame rails, transmission, narrow floor, doors, tubs, bumpers and hardtop, leaving the visible wheel-arch gaps clear. A 0.12 m suspension lift gives the frame about 0.44 m of parked clearance, retaining the existing sag and bump travel. Centre-of-mass and intake positions compensate for the lift to preserve rollover and flooding behavior.
- Each wheel samples nine points along its circular tyre profile, aligned with steering. The best supported point becomes Rapier's contact ray; raising that ray by the circular profile offset keeps the reported suspension length at the real hub. Both leading and trailing samples work in forward and reverse. Steep side faces remain solid obstacles for the bump-stop colliders. Off-centre samples require ground beneath the hub within suspension reach, so tyres do not bridge a deep void by reaching across to its far lip.
- Camp log collision uses the same seven-sided horizontal cylinder, dimensions, orientation and ground base as the rendered log. The former square collision box caught tyres at invisible corners.
- Headless regressions cross the full-height camp log forwards and backwards, with both wheels or one wheel per axle, at a target 0.6 m/s and under 1.5 m/s throughout. They also cover staggered rock ledges, a tall impassable obstacle, clearance beneath a wheel arch, and both actual River Flat Camp logs. Existing hill, rollover, washout, plank, winch and flooding checks remain in place.

### Crawl controls and camp practice course

- Front and rear differential locks are independent (Z/X; gamepad LB menu). Engagement requires at most 5 km/h; unlocking always works. Locked axle drive is capped by the sum of its tyres' available grip, with torque apportioned within each tyre's capacity. Wheel angular travel and extra spin stay coupled on a locked axle. A front locker reduces the available steering angle to 72%, representing its resistance to tight turns. Open axles retain the existing behaviour by default.
- Precision crawl (P; gamepad LB menu) regulates driver-requested speed to 6 km/h forwards and 5 km/h backwards at full input; analogue input scales the target for finer control. It can request full drive effort at a stalled obstacle and apply service braking on a descent. Driver braking retains full authority; releasing the controls uses the existing engine/hold brakes. The normal throttle and gearbox remain available with precision mode off.
- HUD buttons show both lockers and precision mode and accept mouse clicks. Paused menus, tool placement and free camera suppress vehicle commands; menu presses cannot become locker commands after returning to driving. Gamepad crawler actions use the LB radial menu, preserving D-pad camera zoom and right-stick recenter. Simulation snapshots include the two locker states and precision mode.
- Camp Rock Course is a new discoverable feature about 40 m east of camp, on a cleared gravel flat. Three signed lines use low steps, alternating cross-axle slabs and taller/off-camber ledges. Slabs share one geometry definition for rendering and collision; coloured timber markers identify each entrance and exit. Nearby hints use the active device's control glyphs. Headless driving tests cross the easy line with open diffs and the other two with lockers, using precision crawl throughout.

### Low-range articulation, camera views and Granite Link

- Low range blends in 6 cm extra suspension rest length and travel, leaving the full-compression position unchanged. Spring stiffness changes with the expected static sag (gravity / four springs), keeping parked height within 5 mm. Anti-roll resistance drops to 18% and damping rises to control movement. High range retains its original support. The visible solid axles and dampers already follow the simulated wheel hubs, so the added flex is visible without a separate animation.
- On two diagonal 22 cm blocks, the low-range setup keeps all four tyres loaded (minimum over 900 N), where high range lifts one tyre. Grounded-wheel load still determines available traction; the suspension change does not grant grip to airborne tyres. The model remains a ray-cast suspension approximation, with visible solid axles rather than articulated rigid axle bodies.
- C / View cycles follow, low front-left, low front-right and close rear views. V / R3 returns to follow and restores its previous distance. The same terrain clearance, obstruction checks, level horizon and manual orbit apply to every preset. LB + R3 cycles on a gamepad and cancels any open radial selection; ordinary R3 still recentres. Debug free camera moves to backtick to keep C consistent.
- Granite Link is a curved 70 m technical track beside camp, with split rocks, a 10-degree shelf and granite ledges. Its wider 86 m gravel bypass joins both ends. Amber and green timber markers distinguish the routes; nearby HUD hints describe the current section. Two marked boulders provide winch anchors. Both technical and bypass routes are driven end-to-end in both directions in the physics tests, with open diffs on the bypass.
- Trail slabs use clipped corners and bevelled top edges. One vertex definition supplies the render triangles and the convex collision hull; a ray comparison checks the top, bevels and clipped corners. This avoids introducing another visible-gap collision problem.

### Granite Crawl rock obstacles (since replaced by Granite Spur, below)

- The Granite Crawl replaces the earlier four challenges (V-Notch, Log Steps, Boulder Gates, Sidewinder Shelf) with six rock obstacles graded easy to extreme: Rock Garden, Granite Step-ups, Crevice Run, Boulder Field, Off-camber Slab and The Rock Face. Rock is the only obstacle material; there are no logs on this loop. They follow the same loop and keep the same link layout: each obstacle has a separate gravel bypass and a winch anchor, and short green links join entries and exits. Vehicle handling is unchanged.
- Stones are authored per trail section as boulders (seeded faceted ellipsoids with flattened crowns), slabs (bevelled blocks, buried deeper when tilted) or ridges (a top profile along the track with per-station cross-slope). Each stone becomes a convex hull built in the game; the same vertices and triangles feed both rendering and Rapier's convex-hull collider, so there are no visible gaps. Ridges follow the ground along their length, so long slabs sit on sloping ground instead of floating or forming a wall at one end.
- The obstacles sit on the hill's natural 5–8% grade. The earlier per-obstacle flat pads levelled each obstacle to its own average height, which left 0.5–0.8 m terrain steps where neighbouring pads and tracks met, so they were removed. Challenge tracks also use lighter profile smoothing so their ends match at junctions.
- Grades were tuned by driving each obstacle headlessly with open diffs, rear locker, both lockers and high range. The Rock Garden and Crevice Run pass with open diffs; the Boulder Field stalls open diffs and takes about 60% longer on the rear locker alone than with both; the Boulder Field and Rock Face cannot be climbed in high range. Splitting the Rock Face into offset halves twists the body; a larger offset made open diffs fail but put the locked vehicle near rollover, so the face's difficulty comes from height and steepness.
- Physics checks drive every obstacle and bypass in both directions, both complete loops without teleporting, and the grade differences above. A ray comparison checks a stone's collider against its visible faces, and every trail stone is checked to be a closed hull that is buried in the ground. The six discoverable obstacles bring the total to 22 places.
- Clustered discovered names on the paper map move to clear positions around their icons, with thin leader lines.

### Engine sound

- **The engine is synthesised on the audio thread.** The first engine was a fixed oscillator waveform shaped by filters, and it sounded like a buzzer. It is replaced by an AudioWorklet (`src/audio/engine/`) that builds the sound sample by sample the way an engine does, modelled on the 4.2 L inline six of a 1970s 40 Series: six exhaust pulses per two crank turns in firing order 1-5-3-6-2-4; per-cylinder manifold delays and strengths plus cycle-to-cycle variation (the six's burble and the idle lope); a head pipe, muffler and tailpipe whose resonances stay fixed while the firing rate sweeps through them (so the exhaust drones at some revs, as real ones do); intake roar with throttle, valve-train tick, fan noise, overrun misfires and pops, a rev limiter, and misfires while the intake takes in water. Pulse length is set in time and limited in crank angle, so idle is separate pops and high revs a continuous roar.
- **The firing rate is now a six's** (three per crank turn, 40 Hz at idle), and the browser test checks for that.
- **Starter and catch:** the starter cranks with a dip on each compression stroke, then the engine catches, flares to about 1350 rpm and settles. It plays when sound first starts and after a flood stall.
- **Transfer-case whine** is part of the same synthesiser: two gear meshes pitched to the transfer input shaft (wheel turns × final drive × range ratio), at full level in low range and 30% in high, louder under load.
- **Fallback:** where AudioWorklet isn't available (or the module fails to load) the old oscillator engine plays. Levels were matched so the mix balance is unchanged (idle ≈ 0.025–0.03 RMS at the output, loaded ≈ 0.1).
- **Checking without listening:** `scripts/engine-lab.ts` renders idle, crawling load, a full-throttle stall, cruise, redline, overrun, low-range whine, start-up, a rev sweep and a flooded sputter to WAV files and prints RMS, peaks, the strongest spectral peaks and octave-band levels. The tuning targets were a spectrum falling from the firing harmonics (60–250 Hz) to about −20 dB at 2 kHz, enough energy at 250 Hz–1 kHz for laptop speakers, and about 15 dB between idle and a full-load stall.

### Drivetrain: engine and torque converter

- **Throttle sets torque through airflow, not a fixed share of full torque.** Before, force was full torque × throttle, so any throttle eventually reached the range's top speed. Now part throttle limits the air the engine can draw per second, so its torque falls as the revs rise and each pedal position settles at a speed. The full-throttle curve is unchanged and is treated as net flywheel torque.
- **Friction and pumping losses** (15 + 0.01·rpm + 0.016·rpm·(1 − throttle) Nm, about 80 Nm closed-throttle at 2500 rpm, as for a 4.2 L petrol six) give engine braking that rises with revs. A flywheel of 0.3 kg·m² makes the revs build over about a quarter of a second.
- **A fluid torque converter** joins the engine to the gearbox: pump torque ∝ (engine rpm² − turbine rpm²), stalling near 2000 rpm at full throttle. Torque multiplication is a mild 1.15× at stall rather than a typical 2×: with 2× the high-range stall force rose to about 10 kN and high range climbed 30°, which SPEC §6.2 rules out (the Staircase must need low range). Maximum wheel force stays close to before (high ≈ 6.6 kN, low ≈ 16.5 kN). In gear at idle the converter creeps (about 4 km/h in low range on flat dirt); an idle governor holds the revs around 680 rpm under that load.
- **Coasting downhill the automatic holds its gear** (it only upshifts on throttle), so engine braking doesn't vanish on a descent.
- **Hill-start assist:** pulling away on a slope, the brakes stay on until the converter delivers enough drive to hold the car, for at most 1.2 s. Rapier's wheels ignore the brake whenever an engine force is set, so the assist holds the car on the brakes and applies no drive while it's on. The no-input hold brake is now the full service-brake force (it stands for the driver's foot on the pedal); at 7 kN it couldn't hold 1.6 t on 30°.
- **Pedals follow the selected gear.** W used to brake whenever the car rolled backwards. If the car slips back down a rock while in a forward gear (below 1.5 m/s), W now drives forwards against the roll, as the accelerator would in a real car; it still brakes when the car is reversing in reverse gear or rolling back fast. S mirrors this.
- **Precision crawl stops when released:** with creep, letting go would otherwise idle on at walking pace. Crawl control brakes to a stop instead, which is how real crawl-control systems behave.
- **While the winch pulls with no throttle, the gearbox sits in neutral**, so creep neither runs the car up its own cable nor drags against it.
- **Grades re-checked.** The new drivetrain delivers more steady force at part throttle, which let open diffs through the Boulder Field and made a rear locker alone as quick as both. An open axle now passes 10% of its drive to the grounded tyre when one tyre has no grip (was 35%; a real open diff passes almost none), and the front locker limits steering to 85% (was 72%, which made the locked car's line through the boulders worse than the rear-only car's). Open diffs stall in the Boulder Field again and the rear locker alone takes about 47% longer than both. All other grades, bypasses and loops pass unchanged. `scripts/drivetrain-lab.ts` and `scripts/crawl-lab.ts` print the measurements.

### Tyre contact on ledges (tried and reverted)

- The ray-cast wheels place their support on the highest point the tyre's lower arc meets, so the lip of a box ledge pushes straight up and never back: climbing a step costs no effort. A swept-disc contact (Rapier `castShape` of a thin cylinder down each suspension leg, with Rapier's ray aimed through the true contact) plus a correction force along the edge's radial normal was built and tested. On chamfered rocks and logs Rapier's own normal already leans with the face (its suspension force follows the hit normal, scaled by 1/cos), so the extra correction mostly mattered on sharp box corners. The swept contact made the authored obstacles behave very differently (tall boulders lost the flat-top support the trails were designed around, camp logs and course lines stalled), and retuning every obstacle around it was out of proportion for this change. The sampled contact stays; the experiment is recorded here so a later pass can start from it. A full-width cylinder was also rejected: with solid axles the tyres stay flat on the ground while the body rolls, so a cylinder tilting with the chassis wrongly moved the contact to the tyre's shoulder and stopped rollovers.

### Rock-crawling sounds, gauges, driver view and scored runs

- **Chassis contact is measured, not guessed.** After each step the vehicle sums the contact impulses on its body colliders and the sliding speed at the contact (`Vehicle.bodyContact`). The scrape sound needs both pressure and sliding; a new contact over 120 N·s is a knock (a sim event, like wheel impacts). In the Boulder Field a locked run gives about a dozen knocks and 3 s of scraping; the easier obstacles give none.
- **Squeal** is a tyre layer for rock and timber driven by wheelspin, with its pitch and level re-randomised every frame for stick-slip chatter. **Spring squeaks** are scheduled from the summed suspension movement below 4 m/s. Range changes, air lockers (click and hiss) and selecting drive or reverse have their own one-shots.
- **Crawl gauges** are a canvas in the HUD's paper style, redrawn only when their rounded values change. Roll is right-side-down positive; a tyre's shade is its load relative to a quarter of the vehicle's weight.
- **Driver view** is the fifth camera preset: the eye sits in the right-hand seat (0.38, 1.24, 0.3 in vehicle space), the view follows the body's full orientation, and orbit input turns the head within limits. The near plane drops to 5 cm in this view. The tinted glass is hidden from inside: drawn full-screen it hid the trail and was very slow in the software renderer. Heard from the seat, the vehicle's sounds pass through a gentle low-pass (1.6 kHz) at 85% level, as through the body.
- **Scored runs** (`src/sim/crawlRuns.ts`) are headless and opt-in by driving the technical line, so the relaxed exploration is unchanged. Backing up counts when the car drops 0.5 m back from its furthest point, and again only after moving 0.4 m forward; knocks within 0.8 s count once. Personal bests are an optional field in the existing version-1 save, so older saves still load.

### Steering wheel and dash instruments

It is now square to the column (about 59° from horizontal), slightly lower and closer, so the gauges show through its upper half. It turns 13° per degree of front-wheel steer, about 1¼ turns each way at lock, clockwise for a right turn.

### Granite Spur 4WD obstacles

- **Why the Granite Crawl went.** Its six obstacles sat on ground that rose only about 4 m over 120 m, as straight 26 m runs of rocks on open grass. Nothing made you drive over the rocks, the off-camber slab had no drop beside it, and the Rock Face was a dome you could drive round. Real obstacles get their difficulty from the ground: steep climbs and descents, cut gullies, side-cuts above a drop. The new loop is built into a hill and mixes materials, as High Country tracks do (the user chose a mixed loop over rock only).
- **What the obstacles are based on.** Victorian High Country tracks: earth drainage humps (whoa-boys) on steep fire trails that can leave a car on its belly; rock steps on steep pinches like Billy Goat Bluff, where coming down is harder; long shale sidlings like Blue Rag Range; washed-out clay descents with erosion ruts you straddle; fallen trees. Rock obstacles follow the Rubicon's Little Sluice (a gully packed with boulders bigger than the tyres) and Moab's Escalator (a V-notch chute where the line zigzags and a front tyre lifts). Technique hints come from the same driving advice: approach at a slight angle so three wheels stay down, straddle ruts, never turn downhill on a side slope, crawl down on engine braking.
- **The hill.** Granite Spur is a dome 18 m high and about 180 × 135 m (`spurHill`), with a level summit. A gully (a negative ridge) runs below The Sidling so its low side drops away; a tor stands on the east shoulder. It sits where the old loop was, between Granite Link and the Mine track, and stays clear of the Washout Gully.
- **Fire trail instead of per-obstacle bypasses.** On a hill, a separate bypass for each obstacle meant a switchback per climb, and the bypasses crossed each other. Instead a graded gravel fire trail climbs the north face to the summit, rings the summit and rings the foot of the hill, and every obstacle runs between two points on it. `CrawlTrailDef.bypassTrackId` is now optional; the fire trail sections are green links with their own hints. Every fire trail section is driven both ways with open diffs in the tests, and so are the routes round the foot of the hill and up to the summit.
- **New terrain tools.** Tracks can have a `cut` (a cutting or gully floor below the smoothed ground, used by the Creek Bed, Boulder Gully and The Chute) and a `cant` (extra lean across the track, used by The Sidling). Drainage humps and erosion ruts need detail the 1 m heightfield can't hold, so they are built as fine ground patches at 0.25 m (`sim/world/detail.ts`). A patch covers whole 1 m cells and uses the same triangle split, so where it carries no offset it lies exactly on the 1 m triangles; the 1 m samples under it are pushed down below the fine ground for collision and drawing. `Terrain.heightAt` and `normalAt` read the fine ground, physics adds it as a trimesh, and the terrain renderer draws it with the same ground material and blended tints, darker in rut floors. Tests check that the collider matches the drawn surface, that patch edges meet the 1 m ground, and that the hidden surface stays underneath.
- **Logs lie on the ground.** A fallen trunk now rests on the highest ground across the track (the upper hull of the ground profile under its middle), instead of having both ends pinned to the ground. Pinning the ends buried the middle of a log on a crowned track.
- **Grades, measured headlessly** (`scripts/obstacle-lab.ts`; `matrix` mode drives every obstacle with each locker setting, `loop` drives the whole loop with one car):
  - Creek Bed: open diffs and even high range get through; roll stays under 20°.
  - Whoa-boys: open diffs get through; the humps pitch the car to about 33–35°.
  - Rock Steps: open diffs get through in low range; high range stalls.
  - Log Step: everything gets over; angled logs roll the car about 23° as one side climbs first.
  - The Sidling: leans 20–25° in both directions; the washout and rock shelf give the peak.
  - Boulder Gully: about 20 s with both lockers, about 50 s on the rear locker alone; open diffs and high range stall.
  - The Chute: about 15 s with both lockers; rear only, open diffs and high range all stall. Pitch reaches about 40° and roll about 25°.
  - Washout Ruts: roll 20–27° where a wheel drops into a rut. Shallower ruts (0.48 m) and a steeper entry crossing replaced a first version where the main rut caught the right wheels at a shallow angle and tipped the car to 34°.
- **Things found while tuning.**
  - The chute walls first started where the test car spawned, wedging it. They also had a single high wall at a time, which let open diffs climb as fast as lockers. Walls that swap high and low every wheelbase make it truly cross-axle; a 22° inward lean with 0.66/0.26 m walls tipped the car to 51° when it arrived from the gully, so the walls lean 14° and stand 0.62/0.2 m.
  - The test helper toggled lockers once at spawn, before the car on a steep start had slowed, so they never engaged. The tests and lab now settle on the brakes and keep asking for the lockers until they engage, like a player.
  - Signposts beside obstacle ends stood on the fire trail at junctions and stalled the car. Signs now pick a spot clear of every track's graded shoulder and get a small level pad.
  - The fire trail first crossed the Granite Link bypass, ran alongside the Creek Bed's cut bank, and passed under the first drainage hump; each tipped the car. The trail now ends on the bypass, and the Whoa-boys start 10 m along the fire trail from the Creek Bed.
- **Smaller changes.** The course hint follows the nearest crawl trail, so where one obstacle ends and the next begins it names the one you're on. A POI approach comes in along whichever side of its track has room, so short summit tracks give a usable run-up. Obstacle places sit mid-way along (or nearer the fire trail end for the two long ones) and are big enough that driving past on the fire trail finds them. There are 25 places on the map.

### Bush Tucker Man Land Rover 110 Perentie

- **Which vehicle.** Les Hiddins drove three Land Rovers in _Bush Tucker Man_: Army Land Rover 110 Perentie General Service soft tops (registration 48-001, now at the Darwin military museum) in the first two series, and a white Perentie "Carryall" hardtop that never served with the Army in the third. The user chose the Army green soft top.
- **What it copies.** Australian Army Perentie features: the 110 in (2.794 m) wheelbase and 1.486 m track, 7.50R16 tyres on green steel wheels with five studs, coil springs with radius arms, a galvanised chassis (drawn light grey), the spare wheel slung under the rear floor (the Perentie's main difference from British 110s), a heavy bull bar, a full-length canvas tilt with canvas door tops and clear windows, flat Army green paint, vent flaps under an upright windscreen, a black slatted grille between the headlamp panels, an air intake grille on the left wing, Army-pattern rear lamps and a pintle hook for the No. 5 trailer. The plates read 48-001 in a stroked font drawn in code; the grille badge is a plain oval, as on the other vehicle, so no logo artwork is copied. Jerry cans behind the rear wheels, an HF whip aerial, a swag and a tucker box in the tub are period touches rather than details confirmed from the series.
- **Things added for the game.** A winch behind the bull bar and the two recovery boards (strapped on the tilt roof over its hoops) are there because the game's tools need them. Front and rear lockers are kept so every obstacle stays possible; Army Perenties had a centre diff lock only.
- **Drivetrain.** The Isuzu 4BD1 3.9 L diesel (about 70 kW and 245 Nm at 1900 rpm, governed at 3600) drives the LT95A four-speed (4.069, 2.448, 1.505, 1.0) through a 1.174 / 3.32 transfer case to 3.54 axles. The game has no clutch pedal, so the drivetrain model's torque converter stands in for one: no extra torque multiplication to speak of (1.25 at stall), and the gears change themselves. With the brakes held it idles at about 680 rpm; 0–60 km/h takes about 11 s and it cruises at 105 km/h under 3000 rpm.
- **Stability.** The first centre of mass (0.82 m off the ground) rolled the 110 in a full-lock turn at 50 km/h, which the short-wheelbase 4WD survives. At 0.78 m, with roll stiffness standing in for the coils and bushes (no anti-roll bars on early 110s), it rolls at 60 km/h as the other vehicle does.
- **On Granite Spur.** Measured headlessly, the 110 drives every obstacle both ways with both lockers, every fire trail section and both no-lockers routes, and the whole loop, except one: coming down The Chute it grounds its chassis on a wall step and stays there. The Chute's walls change height every 2.3 m, the short-wheelbase 4WD's wheelbase, so with 2.79 m between its axles the 110's belly meets a step as the rear wheels drop. Other lines across the notch fail the same way. It is left as it is: a long wheelbase grounding on a crest is a real limitation, the loop climbs The Chute, and the tests cover the 110 on the loop and the fire trail routes.
- **Engine sound.** The synthesiser now takes any cylinder count from its tuning. The diesel fires four times per two turns, has stronger compression pulses (no throttle plate), cuts fuel on overrun instead of popping, cranks longer, and adds combustion knock: each firing rings two resonances at about 1.5 and 2.9 kHz, loudest at idle and light load. Its 1.3–3.2 kHz band is about six times stronger, against its own low end, than the petrol six's at idle.
- **Swapping vehicles** (pause menu → Vehicle) rebuilds the simulated vehicle, its model, steering rates, driver's seat and engine sound where the old one stood, facing the same way, with the winch let go and placed planks returned. Removing the old Rapier body in the same step as adding the new one left the new body frozen once the world had stepped, so the old body is disabled at once and removed after the next physics step. The vehicle is a new optional field in the version-1 save; older saves start in the short-wheelbase 4WD.

The HUD moves out of the thumbs' way when the controls are showing (a `touch-mode` class on the page): the status strip and course hints go to the top left, the camera buttons to the top right and the crawl gauges to the bottom centre (top right in portrait). The status strip's lock and crawl buttons are hidden then, since the touch grid has the same buttons and shows their state.
- **Input.** The touch controls feed the same `InputManager` as the keyboard and gamepad, as a third device (`touch`): held buttons act like held keys, taps like key presses, and the joystick like the gamepad's left stick (same steering rate). Pushing the joystick most of the way in one direction also counts as one menu step, which chooses winch anchors; it has to come back towards the centre before the next step (`TouchStick`, tested without a browser). USE sends what E does on the keyboard (winch, pick up planks, confirm). IN, OUT and UPRIGHT only show when they can do something, and in plank and anchor modes the pedals become BACK and USE.
- **When they show.** On a coarse pointer (phones and tablets), and whenever the screen is touched; a key press or gamepad hides them again, so a touchscreen laptop or a phone with a controller drives as before. They hide while the map, pause menu or tool menu covers the screen. The tool menu opened from TOOLS stays open until an option or the space around it is tapped, since there is no held button to let go of.
- **Camera.** A finger dragged on the view orbits the camera (mouse drag ignored touch before). Any finger can start the drag, because the finger on the joystick may be the browser's primary one. The user asked for pinch to zoom: a second finger on the view turns the drag into a pinch, and the change in the gap between the two fingers moves the camera in or out (`touchTuning.pinchZoomPerPx`, within the usual zoom limits). When one finger lifts, the other does nothing until it is lifted too, so the camera doesn't jump.
- **Performance.** A phone or tablet with no saved settings starts on the Low preset (`touchTuning.defaultQuality`); the pause menu can still raise it.
- Screenshots of the phone layout are in `docs/screenshots/touch/`.

### v0.11: wet braking and puddles

- **Why braking barely changed in the rain.** Measured from 60 km/h on dirt, the cars stopped in 15.8 m dry and 17.1 m in rain, although the rain takes about a third of the tyres' grip. Rapier's ray-cast vehicle lets a wheel's brake push up to about twice its sideways grip limit before it slides (the Bullet-style forward friction factor), so the brakes were never limited by grip.
- **Grip-limited brakes.** Each wheel's brake force is now capped at that wheel's grip (the same surface, wetness and load limit used for driving) times 1.12, the extra a tyre gives just short of locking. Asking for more locks the wheel: it brakes at three quarters of its peak grip, keeps 30% of its sideways grip (so a car locked up in a turn runs wide) and scrubs like a sliding tyre. Below 1.5 m/s the brakes simply hold the car, so parking and hill holds are unchanged. With that alone, the rear wheels locked in dry braking (the weight moves forward) and the stop got longer (18.2 m); real cars fit a load-sensing valve for this, so while the car is moving the rear brakes are limited to 0.95 times the rear wheel's load, and dry stops are 16–17 m with no wheel locked. The valve leaves the handbrake and the brakes holding a stopped car alone: applied to them it let the short-wheelbase 4WD roll back on Granite Spur's Rock Steps in high range, take a run-up and get further up than it should.
- **Measured, from 60 km/h.** Short-wheelbase 4WD: 17.1 m dry, 18.9 m after rain, 22.9 m in rain. Perentie: 16.3, 18.3 and 19.5 m. Both stay straight. Parked on a 25° slope, the hold brake still stops the cars within 0.3–0.4 m in any weather.
- **Puddles.** After rain and in rain, `addPuddles` presses shallow bowls (2.4–5.5 m long, up to 8 cm deep after rain and 12 cm in rain) into flat stretches of the dirt and gravel tracks and fills them to just below their lowest rim, using the creek's water grid, so the existing water physics (drag, wheel depth, wading), sound and splash work in them. It runs on the built world before the physics and meshes read the ground, with a fixed seed per map, so the same puddles come back. Rock tracks, faint tracks, tracks with humps, erosion or steps, the park's obstacle lines and the places where the loop meets them are left dry, so the stations play the same in any weather. The park gets about 15 puddles after rain and 50 in rain; the High Country about 10 and 35.
- **Drawing them.** Creek water is drawn pulled slightly towards the camera so it never flickers against the creek bed. On a flat track that offset let puddle water show through ground a centimetre or two above it, so puddles came out as square blocks along the grid. Puddles now use their own material without the offset (told apart from the creek by distance from it), stand 2 cm below their rim, and are tinted muddy brown. Splashes now start at 2 cm of water instead of 5 cm, so a puddle splashes.
- A puddle on the park loop in the rain: `docs/screenshots/park/puddle-rain.png`.

### v0.12: Crawler Creek gate trail and the Camel Trophy 4WD

- **What was asked.** The user likes RC crawler courses (a trail of numbered gates with tight switchbacks, bridges, climbs and mud holes) and asked for one at full size, with gate scoring possibly in a later version, and for a Camel Trophy car, suggesting the short-wheelbase 4WD in Camel Trophy livery.
- **One loop of gates.** Crawler Creek is a 768 m map, like Ironbark 4WD Park, with a single 1.3 km loop from the Trailhead in twelve sections (`data/gateTrail.ts`). Each section is its own track and crawl trail, so the existing hints ("Mud Holes · Gates 7–10"), markers, signs, places on the paper map and scored runs all work per section. Sections join end to end; a test checks they meet within 1 cm and at the same graded height, and the Camel Trophy 4WD drives the whole loop in one go.
- **Gates.** A gate is data (`GateDef`: number, track, distance along it, offset and width), turned into its two flag positions when the world is built (`GateInfo`). The flags are drawn but don't collide, as a real gate flag would bend out of the way. Nothing is scored yet; a later version can count a gate as passed when the car crosses the line between its flags, in order, and a flag as hit when the body comes within a few centimetres of it. The flags are red on the left of the way through and blue on the right; the number is on a board on the red flag's stake, facing the approach. All the flags are one mesh and all the number boards another, with one texture of every number.
- **Bridges** are a new track feature (`TrackBridgeDef`): a deck the full width, or two planks one under each wheel track, optionally with kerbs and a ramp up and over. The ground under a bridge is left as it is (no grading), so the creek, a bog or flat ground stays under the deck, and the deck runs straight between the graded track at each end, standing 5 cm proud with a short lip. Decks, kerbs, stringers, headstocks, posts and sills are box-shaped hulls whose vertices are both drawn and used as convex colliders, like the trail rocks, so the tyres run on exactly the boards drawn; a test casts rays along every bridge and finds the deck within 5 mm and timber underfoot. The deck tops are drawn board by board on the hull's own top face. Putting a car down (a teleport, a vehicle swap, the debug panel) uses the deck height where there is one, not the ground under it. Wheel ruts and trail markers skip bridges, and the paper map draws the usual bridge sign.
- **Knuckle Ridge** is an escarpment rather than a hill: a straight face 90 m wide and 26 m high for the switchbacks, a level top for the summit rocks, and a short, steep east end for The Drop (24° on the graded track, nearer 30° in the car over the ruts).
- **Switchbacks.** On a slope s, a semicircular hairpin on the natural ground climbs on average 0.64 × s whatever its radius, which on the ridge's face was a 25–30% turn. Real switchbacks grade the turn flatter and cut and fill the hillside, and the track does the same: a new `even` option grades the whole climb at one even 7.5% while the legs slant up the face to keep the cut and fill to a metre or two, with wider shoulders (3.5 m) for the banks. The hairpins have an 8 m radius on a 4.2 m bench; with a 6.5 m radius on a 3.8 m bench the test driver cut a hairpin, left the bench and rolled the short-wheelbase 4WD. They're gated just before and after each apex towards the outside of the turn.
- **Grades, measured headlessly** with both vehicles in low range: every section goes with open diffs except the Rock Garden, whose two packed boulder fields stop both vehicles with open diffs and go with both lockers. The first Mud Holes tipped the short-wheelbase 4WD 36° in a "moderate" section and the first Drop pitched it 36°, so the holes are shallower (0.3–0.46 m) and the ridge's east end longer. Summit Rocks passed with open diffs and is graded moderate.
- **Camel Trophy 4WD.** The user's suggestion was taken: the short-wheelbase 4WD with the same chassis, drivetrain, cab and sounds (`data/camel.ts`). The Camel Trophy's own vehicles were Land Rovers; the 1986 event ran in Australia, from Cooktown to Darwin, in Land Rover 90s. The livery follows the event cars: Land Rover's Sandglow yellow on the body, hardtop, window frames and wheels (the wheel colour wasn't confirmed from a source), black "CAMEL / TROPHY" lettering and a camel on both doors and the bonnet, "AUSTRALIA" on the rear quarters, four round lamps on a bar across the front of the roof rack and a snorkel up the left windscreen pillar. The lettering is block capitals drawn as strokes and the camel is a traced outline, both built in code (`render/camelLivery.ts`) like the Perentie's number plates, so the decals take dirt and dents with the paint and no artwork is copied. The snorkel is real: the engine's intake moves to its head, about 2 m off the ground, so the car wades water that stalls the plain short-wheelbase 4WD. The aerial moves to the right wing to make room.

### v0.14: night and headlights

- **What was asked.** The user asked for switchable headlights that improve visibility at dusk and in the dark. Dusk was the darkest time of day, so Night was added to the welcome screen as well.
- **Night.** A fifth clear-sky preset (`data/environment.ts`): the "sun" is a high, faint blue moon in the north-east (intensity 0.42 against 2.3 at dusk), a dark blue hemisphere light and haze, and a near-black sky. The sky shader gains stars (one at most per cell of a fine grid on the dome, random brightness) and a cloud brightness, so clouds stay dark at night instead of glowing white. Overcast weather at night darkens towards near-black greys and hides the stars. The birds keep quiet at night and crickets chirp: one high tone pulsed in short trills, quieter in the cab and not in the rain.
- **Switching.** N cycles off, low beam, high beam (BeamNG uses N for lights; H is the horn, L the range). The gamepad has no free button, so it's in the tool menu, which now has seven options; the touch screen has a LIGHTS button in the top row, where rarely used buttons sit. No shadows: a spot light's shadow map would draw the scene again for each lamp.
- **Tuning.** The first strengths lit the grass and rocks well but hardly the ground: low beam meets flat ground at a slant, which spreads its light thin, while upright tufts face it. Low beam is now 700 at a 9° dip and high beam 1700 at 3°, with a gentler fall-off than physical (decay 1.2): low beam lights the ground about 20 m ahead and high beam reaches the trees about 40 m away. The lenses glow (emissive 2.4 low, 3.4 high), the tail lamps glow with the lights on and brighter under braking, and in the driver's seat the dial needles glow and a blue high-beam lamp lights on the dash.

### v0.15: mechanical damage

- **What was asked.** The user asked for mechanical damage and chose to make it a setting: Off, Mild and Realistic. Mild is the default: damage slows you down but never strands you, which suits a relaxed game. Realistic can leave you with a flat tyre or a dead engine.
- **What breaks.** The health values in `VehicleCondition` (SPEC §11) were already read by the physics through multipliers, all 1 until now. `sim/vehicle/damage.ts` lowers them: tyres (grip, a smaller rolling radius so the corner drops, drag at that corner that pulls the car), suspension corners (softer spring so the corner sags; a bent front corner knocks its wheel out of line, up to 4°, pulling towards its side) and the engine (power). A cut tyre loses its air over a few seconds rather than at once (14 s down to half in Mild, 6 s flat in Realistic). Mild keeps tyres at half, corners at 60% and the engine at 50% health (70% power); Realistic goes to flat tyres and a wrecked engine, which cranks but never runs.
- **Measuring a hard landing.** The first version used the suspension's compression speed, which the sound already used for thumps. Driving the trail tests with damage on showed it spikes to 4–18 m/s at 3–8 km/h whenever a rock step rises under a slowly rolling wheel (the ray-cast wheel's ground jumps up in one step), and 18 of the trail tests broke the car. The landing is now measured as how fast that corner of the body was falling when its wheel landed or bottomed (`Vehicle.wheelLandings`), which a step rising under a crawling wheel doesn't produce. Crawling up, over and down every trail and park line with both vehicles measured at most 3.6 m/s; a tyre is cut above 4.4 m/s on rock (about a metre's drop) and a corner bends above 5.6 m/s (about 1.6 m).
- **The engine.** A crash is a sudden change of the body's speed (over 6 m/s in one step) pointing backwards along the body: something hit the nose. Chassis knocks hurt it too, but belly scrapes on the trails measured up to about 4400 N·s, so only knocks over 8000 count (a 50 km/h crash into a wall is about 20000); a crash is not also counted as a knock. Upside down with the engine running costs 6% a second. In Realistic, a flooding stall costs 35% (water drawn into the cylinders).
- **Checked against the trails.** With Realistic damage forced on, the trail, crawl course, park, Crawler Creek, Camel Trophy, POI, tools and wet-weather tests (192) all pass, and the only damage is from the two tests meant to cause it: the Deep Crossing stall and the rollover. The Camel Trophy car's drive round the whole of Crawler Creek now runs with Realistic damage and expects none.
- **Repairs.** A field repair (Mild) or fitting the one spare (Realistic) comes from the tool menu, needs the car stopped and fairly level, and holds it on the handbrake while it runs (6 s and 8 s). Driving into a campsite, or Return to last campsite, puts everything right and the spare back. Changing the setting brings damage within what the new setting allows (Off puts everything right).
- **Showing it.** A message names the part and corner; a small top-down car outline in the status strip colours each corner and the engine amber or red; a prompt offers the repair when stopped; a badly hurt engine smokes from under the bonnet; a flat tyre is drawn squashed. Sounds: a crack and hiss for a cut tyre, a crunch for a bent corner, a knock for engine damage and the stall clunk when it's wrecked.
- **The tool menu** now has eight options (headlights in v0.14, repair now). Laid out on the old 120 px circle, neighbouring options overlapped from seven on (v0.14 shipped with that). The options now sit on an oval that widens until no two overlap, and grows taller instead on a phone held upright, always on the screen (`ringRadii`, tested on desktop and both phone orientations); upright phones get slightly narrower options so seven fit side by side at the bottom.

### v0.18: flag touches on Crawler Creek

- **What counts.** A flag is touched when its stake (plus 6 cm for the stake and pennant) is inside the car seen from above: a rectangle as long and wide as the vehicle's body boxes and tyres (`footprintSize`, 4.15 × 1.84 m for the short-wheelbase 4WD, 4.9 × 1.72 m for the Perentie), centred on the car and turned to its heading. Crawler Creek's gates are 3–4 m wide, so driving the middle leaves at least 0.6 m each side. The flags still have no colliders: a real marker flag is a thin stake that bends, and making it solid would stop the car or throw it, so the touch is worked out in `GateCount` rather than by physics.
- **Once per flag per lap.** A car sitting on a flag, or backing up and touching it again, would otherwise count it every step. RC crawler rules often count each touch; one per flag keeps a slow, careful line through a tight gate from piling up points.
- **When a touch belongs to a lap.** The lap starts at the first gate driven through, but the car's nose reaches that gate's flags before its centre crosses the gate, so touches made while no lap is on are kept aside and those on the starting gate are added when the lap starts. In the same way the car's tail can still touch the last gate's flags after its centre has passed, so the lap's result is held until the car is clear of them.
- **Feedback.** A light knock (the chassis knock sound at 0.35), a short "Touched gate 13's red flag" message, the count under the compass ("Gate 14 / 52 · 2 flags", in the accent colour like a missed gate) and the flags in the lap result card.

# Over the Cape

## v0.01: the episode system, fuel and spares, and Episode 1 (the CREB Track)

The requirements came from a question-and-answer session with the owner; SPEC.md §1 lists them.

**Starting point.** The repository is a copy of 4WD Simulator v0.18. The three maps (High Country, Ironbark 4WD Park, Crawler Creek) are no longer in the game; their data moved to `tests/fixtures/maps` so the 370-odd physics tests that drive their obstacles still run. The generic systems they used (crawl trails, scored runs, gates) stay in the engine for later episodes. Their browser tests went with them; the browser tests were rewritten around the CREB and the episode flow. The save key is new (`overthecape.save.v1`), so the two games don't share saves on the same site.

**No people.** The owner asked for vehicles only and no real names, so there are no characters, presenters or TV show names in the game. Real historical figures appear in the history notes as history (Dalrymple, Richard Daintree). Les Hiddins and the TV series were taken off the Perentie's descriptions; it is now simply the Army Land Rover 110 Perentie.

**Map size and length.** The owner chose big maps with the real landmarks in order and obstacles at full size, distances shortened. The CREB map is 3072 m square (3073² height samples, about 10 s to generate in Node, 8–17 s to load in the browser) with about 5.25 km of track, 4.75 km on the main route. The aim was 8–12 km; the range only fits so much winding before the grades get too steep, so the first map is shorter and the time and fuel are tuned to it. Later maps can use more of their area.

**How the map was made.** The route runs south to north like the real track. The range is one long stretched dome along the route with a peak at the north end (the lookout), a spur for Big Red, a knob for Bob's Hill and high country west and north-west for the creeks to come from. Heights were fixed with `scripts/route-check.ts` (grade per 4 m, ground under each waypoint, water against ground for every creek point) and a height table round each obstacle, then every leg was driven with `scripts/creb-drive.ts`. Lessons kept in the code:

- A track pinned to a height has to be pinned to the _carved_ ground (creeks lower their banks), or it digs a ditch or builds a bank where it meets the water.
- Wide rivers need a wide carved valley (`valley`); with the default 34 m the bank of a 26 m-wide river ended in a step.
- Junctions must be pinned to the same height on both tracks; a 1 m difference rolled the car at the Big Red bypass.
- A creek's water must stay below the ground on both sides along its whole length, or the carve raises it into an aqueduct; the CREB test checks this.

**Episode 1 accuracy.** Each history note is limited to what the sources in docs/HISTORY.md say; where sources disagree (the Lions Den's first licence, Jack Ross's age, the CREB power line's dates) the note leaves the disputed detail out. Big Red and Bob's Hill are named in trip reports as the hardest climbs, with a bypass round Big Red; the descent to Wujal Wujal is concreted with grooves; the Daintree crossing is about a metre deep with a shallower line downstream. Names of the side creeks (Roaring Meg, Donovan's) come from trip reports; their exact positions on the track are not documented, so their order is a best guess. The northern road from Wujal Wujal to the Lions Den is called the Bloomfield Track here, as many guides do.

**Weather and time.** The story starts the CREB at 12:30 pm, after overnight rain, with a 6:15 pm deadline (dark, in June). The clock runs at 5.5 game seconds a real second, so the afternoon lasts about 63 minutes of driving; the autopilot needs 18 minutes, so a first run at two or three times that arrives with time to spare. Fuel burns 18 times faster than the engine's real use, because the map is about a twentieth of the real 100-odd km; the autopilot's run uses 33 L of petrol, and the load is 50 L plus a 20 L can.

**Moving sun.** Rather than add new presets, `environmentAt` blends the existing five time-of-day presets pinned to clock times (bearings blend the short way round) and applies the weather as before. The renderer's `setEnvironment` already existed; it is called every three game minutes. The vehicle model is rebuilt once when dusk turns the headlight beams on.

**Fuel model.** Burn comes from the power the engine is really making (combustion torque × speed), so idling, crawling and wheelspin cost what they should; petrol and diesel differ by efficiency and energy per litre. The engine needs fuel to run, like it needs to be dry and unwrecked.

**Tyres.** The owner asked for blowouts, swapping tyres and limited spares. Realistic damage now tells a puncture (slow leak, a plug fixes it) from a torn sidewall (fast, needs a spare), and spinning on rock wears through. The spare is now a count. Camps no longer give back spares: what the episode loads is what you have.

**Tool menu.** It holds at most eight options without overlapping on a phone, so Winch and Detach winch became one slot to make room for Pour in a jerry can.

**Restart, not return.** "Return to last campsite" became "Restart from last camp", which also restores the clock and the load from when you got there. Otherwise a free trip back to camp would undo the stakes. Changing vehicle mid-episode does the same.

**Vegetation.** Rainforest is drawn with three new species in the same style as the eucalypts: canopy trees on buttress roots with broad-leaf cards, fan palms (round pleated leaves on stalks) and tree ferns (arching fronds). The leaf texture became four quarters. Rainforest is about 1.25 times as dense as the eucalypt forest; on Medium the start view draws about 750 000 triangles.

**Welcome screen.** Episodes 2–7 are listed by name and route as coming later, as a compact list, so Drive stays near the top of the screen. The difficulty is chosen there too, because it decides how an episode can end.

## v0.04: cuttings laid back

**What was reported.** The owner sent a screenshot of the Land Rover reversing up the concrete descent: the rear of the car was buried in a clay bank, and it drove through it as if it were flat. On a map screenshot they marked where it happened: the bend about 40 m down from the lookout. A browser replay of reversing up the track from below reproduced it: the Land Rover stuck on the wall of the cutting at (15, −496), tilted 40°, with the bank showing through the body.

**Why.** Where a track runs below the natural ground, its banks blended back to the hillside over the track's `shoulder`, 3–4 m on every CREB track. Several tracks run 3–8 m below the ground: the concrete descent (up to 5.6 m at the bend, 8.6 m further down), the Big Red bypass, Bob's Hill and the range top. That made near-vertical walls 40–70° steep either side of a narrow floor. A vehicle that strayed off the line, as one reversing round a bend does, climbed the wall and hung up on it. The Land Rover's body boxes are narrower than its drawn body (the rear tub box is 1.2 m wide against a body of about 1.7 m), so the bank also showed through its sides. The boxes were left as they are: with gentle banks there is nothing for the body to sink into.

**Batters.** A track can now have a `batter`: the steepest average slope of its banks. Wherever the track runs deeper below (or higher above) the natural ground than its shoulder allows at that slope, the bank widens. The width is found at each point along the track, held over the next 6 m either way and eased along the track, so the deepest cut gets its full width and the banks don't jag. It stops at 14 m, so a cut across a hillside steeper than the batter doesn't reshape the whole slope. The CREB's tracks use 1 in 2 (27° on average, about 37° at the steepest part of the bank). At the bend the bank on the uphill side now rises at up to 31° from a wide floor, where before it was 50°. The maps 4WD Simulator's physics tests use have no batter and are unchanged.

**Checked.**

- No point along any CREB track has ground 1.5 m above it 3 m out from both edges. There were 132 such points before, on Big Red, the bypass, Bob's Hill, the range top and the concrete. This is now a unit test.
- A unit test reverses the Land Rover up the bend from below. On the v0.02 map it reached 40° of tilt within 6 s; now it stays under 30° and gets past the spot. (Reversing in a straight line, it still meets the bank about 10 m further up where the track turns, as it would on a real road.)
- All three vehicles still drive the whole route in the wet, in the same time and on the same fuel as before (18.1 minutes, 33 L petrol, 24 L diesel). The other CREB tests (legs joining at the same height, level camps, creeks below their banks, the autopilot drives) all pass.

## v0.05: running water, rough tracks, and no wheelspin without the engine

The owner said the game wasn't hard enough: the roads should be rougher and harder to drive quickly, the water should flow and push the vehicle, and the wheels kept spinning when the tank was empty and the accelerator was pressed.

**Wheelspin with no engine.** A wheel bogged on loose ground (mud, gravel, grass, dirt, a creek bed) was made to spin whenever the accelerator was down and the car was nearly stopped. That spin drives the wheel animation, mud sink, dirt, the tyre sound and wear on rock, and it never asked whether the engine was running. It now needs the engine running, so with an empty tank, while cranking or with a flooded engine the pedal turns nothing.

**Running water.** The spec planned the river current for Episode 4. The owner chose to have it now, strong enough to carry the vehicle away, ending the episode on Mild and Realistic (with damage off the car just drifts).

- **Where the water runs.** A creek's `flow` (or a point's) is its speed mid-channel in m/s; it runs from the first point to the last, slowing to half at the edges and to nothing a metre and a half up the bank. On the CREB: the Daintree 2 m/s (high after the overnight rain), Roaring Meg 2, Donovan's 1.8, the Little Annan 1.6, the Bloomfield 1.5. `WaterCurrent` finds the nearest creek segment from a 16 m grid; the vehicle asks once a step, the water mesh once per vertex.
- **Drag against moving water.** The old water drag, with the same coefficients, is worked out against the water's velocity rather than the ground, so standing in a current it pushes. Side-on it is 2.2 times as strong, about a short 4WD's side area over its front.
- **Lift.** Water used only to drag: a car in 1.5 m of water sat on its tyres with full grip, so nothing could push it. Now each body part is lifted by 30% of the water it displaces (the rest leaks in) and each tyre by all of its own (they are sealed). The short-wheelbase 4WD is lifted by about 24% of its weight on the Daintree's gravel bar (0.55 m), 62% in the deep middle (1.05 m) and 85% at 1.3 m. Lift pushes up at each part, and the engine end is the heavy end, so the tail lifts and swings downstream first, as floodwater turns real vehicles.
- **Tyres creep in running water.** Rapier's ray-cast wheels cancel most sideways sliding every step, so a steady sideways push below the point of breaking loose left the car crabbing by only 1–2°: on the bar the current made no difference you could see. In running water over the hubs a tyre now keeps only 15% of that side stiffness when rolling (66% standing still), so the push shows: crossing on the bar takes 8–10° of steering into the current in the short-wheelbase 4WD (about 3° in still water) and about 5° in the Land Rover. Steering in deep running water is slower to bite, which is how it feels.
- **Swept away.** Lifted by 45% of its weight or more and moving downstream faster than 0.5 m/s for 4 s. Under 2 m/s water on flat ground, both vehicles are carried off at 1.3 m deep; at 1.05 m the short-wheelbase 4WD is swung round and carried and the Land Rover holds; at 0.55 m both are pushed but cross. On the Daintree's straight line the car swings round and is shoved 11–13 m downstream onto the bar (which lies downstream), where it stops, flooded unless it has the snorkel; out in the main river (1.4 m) it is swept away in about 4.4 s.
- **The HUD** warns "Deep, fast water — the current is lifting the car" when lifted by over a quarter of its weight in running water, and "The current is taking the car — get to shallow water" while the 4 s run.
- **The look.** Ripples and pale streaks move downstream at the water's speed. Two copies of the pattern slide with the current and cross-fade, each restarting while the other is in full view, so the pattern never stretches; the streaks are drawn out along the current.
- **Browser tests.** The flooding test parked in the deep Daintree at (-1250, 1048). There the car is now swept away (4.4 s) before the engine floods (10 s) on Mild, so it floods on the crossing's deep line pointing upstream instead, where the current doesn't take it. The episode test now also drives into the deep river and checks the "swept" ending.

**Rough tracks.** A track's `rough` adds, to the 1 m ground as it is graded: bumps (about 3 m apart), swells (about 10 m apart), potholes in the wheel lines (0.7–1.4 m across the middle) and washouts (channels 1.2–1.9 m wide cut across the track at 15–50°, so they twist the car). The fine ground-detail patches (0.25 m) would draw them sharper, but building one walks the track's whole bounding box at 0.25 m, far too slow for tracks a kilometre long.

- **On the CREB.** The roads (Upper Daintree Road, the paddock road, the Bloomfield Track): bumps 10 cm, swells 20 cm, 5 potholes per 100 m up to 25 cm deep, a washout per 100 m. The range tracks: bumps 12 cm, swells 15 cm, 3 potholes and 4 washouts per 100 m, washouts up to 30 cm. Big Red and Bob's Hill: bumps 8 cm and 4 washouts per 100 m. The concrete and the river crossing stay smooth. Potholes are tinted dark mud and washouts bare earth so they can be seen coming.
- **Speed.** On the paddock road the short-wheelbase 4WD took about 5 hard suspension hits smooth; rough, 75 at 24 km/h, 290 at 44 km/h and 330 with a little air at 53 km/h, and at 65 km/h it rolled over (it didn't on the smooth road).
- **Strikes.** Suspension compression speed was too noisy to judge a hit by (it spikes past 7 m/s on a smooth road when a wheel touches down), so the generator records each hole's depth on its 1 m samples, and a wheel dropping in makes a strike: the vehicle's speed × the depth, give or take a quarter for how squarely it hits. Over 3.6 the tyre is pinched (a puncture); over 4.8 that corner's suspension also bends by 15%. On a range track the short-wheelbase 4WD took no damage at 37 km/h and two punctures in 460 m at 45; on the roads none at 45 km/h and about one at 52.
- **Fuel.** Every bump takes work, which the dampers turn into heat. The autopilot's clean run of the CREB now uses 46 L of petrol in the short-wheelbase 4WD (35 L on the same route graded smooth; 33 L before this update) and 31 L of diesel in the Land Rover (24 L before). The load stays 50 L and a 20 L can: a careful run still arrives with fuel in hand, with less to spare.

## v0.07: a long, narrow CREB, black soil, deep bogs, a rocky Daintree crossing and flat tyres you can see

**What the owner said.** Difficulty was getting better but needed a bit more. The Daintree crossing should be bumpy and tricky, a line to pick between boulders and holes rather than a straight drive. ## v0.08: a Cape York horizon

**What the owner said.** The mountains in the distance at the edges of the map didn't look like Cape York; replace them with a horizon true to the real country.

**What was there.** Two things made the edges: 170 m border mountains in the terrain itself, with sharp ridged crests and grey scree on their steep faces, and 4WD Simulator's backdrop, three rings of jagged low-poly ridges laid out for the Victorian high country. Grown to clear the CREB's corners, the rings stood 900–1600 m high, so at the Lions Den a pointed blue alpine peak rose behind grey crags. From the middle of the map the rings were beyond the draw distance on every preset, so there the border mountains were the whole horizon.

**The real country.** The CREB runs north through the McDowall Range in the Wet Tropics: rounded ranges under rainforest, not bare peaks. East, between the range and the Coral Sea, are Thornton Peak (1374 m, granite on top), Mt Pieter Botte (1009 m, a granite dome), Mt Sorrow behind Cape Tribulation and, east of the Bloomfield Track, Mt Finnigan (1136 m). The Daintree and the Bloomfield run out to the sea, the Bloomfield into Weary Bay below Wujal Wujal. West, the ranges climb to the flat-topped tablelands of the Great Dividing Range. North of the Lions Den the country dries out to open eucalypt woodland, and Black Mountain (Kalkajaka), heaps of black granite boulders, overlooks the hotel. South of the Daintree are cane and cattle flats and the ranges towards Mossman (Mt Demi, 1159 m).

**The far land.** `MapDef.horizon` describes the country round a map: rolling foothills, a coastline (sea to the east), peaks and massifs (`HillDef` plus bare granite above a share of the height, grey or black), ranges, rivers running on from the map's edge with their water levels (flat floors, cleared or forested), and areas of drier woodland. Heights are about a quarter of the real ones and distances brought in with the map, so the peaks keep their real proportions: Thornton Peak is about 440 m, Mt Finnigan 370, Pieter Botte 330, the tablelands 310, Black Mountain 150. A map without one gets rolling forested hills all round.

`render/horizon.ts` turns it into one mesh: 32 rings round the map's edge, 15 m apart at the edge and kilometres apart near the horizon, out to 32 km, with a vertex every 48 m along the edges and ten round each corner (11,200 vertices, 21,700 triangles; it builds in about 0.2 s). The first ring follows the map's own ground at the edge, a metre under the lowest point within a vertex either way, so no gap shows between them; the far landforms take over over the first 500 m. Forest is drawn as the top of the canopy (22 m over rainforest, 9 m over woodland, 7 m over mangroves), rising over the first 60 m where the map's own trees stand in for it, so the trees at the edge run on into the far forest instead of standing in front of bare ground. Cover is coloured from the palette, mottled like a canopy: rainforest, olive woodland, pale green paddocks on the cleared river flats, dark mangroves on the coast, grey or black granite, rivers, and the sea shading from shallow green to deep blue offshore (a new `sea` colour).

- **Drawn behind everything.** The far land is drawn straight after the sky and before everything else, without writing depth, with the outermost ring first. Seen from anywhere inside the map, distance from the map's edge only grows along a line of sight, so drawing far rings first lets nearer land cover farther land, and everything in the map is drawn over it. Land beyond the camera's far plane is pulled in along the same line of sight to just inside it (the vertex shader scales its position towards the camera), so it looks the same and shows on Low, Medium and High.
- **Fading into the sky.** The world's haze levels off at 82% of the haze colour, which is darker than the sky at the horizon, so the sea met the sky in a hard dark line. Past 2.5 km the far land now fades on into the sky's own horizon colour (about half way by 10 km, 93% at 32 km), so far ranges pale with distance and the sea meets the sky softly.
- **Valleys keep the peaks.** A river's valley cuts into the land no farther than 450 m beyond its floor. With no limit, the gentle sides of the Daintree's flats capped everything for kilometres and flattened Thornton Peak to 74 m. A test checks the named peaks keep their full height.

**The border.** The CREB's border hills are now 80 m (were 170) and `forested`: rounded, with no sharp crests or scree, under forest like the rest of the range. A gap at the north edge opens the view from the Lions Den north to Black Mountain, where the road runs on to Cooktown. Every track stays at least 130 m clear of where the border hills start, so no track or camp moved.

**Tests.** New `tests/horizon.test.ts`: the named peaks keep their height; the sea is east of the whole strip and never west; the Daintree and the Bloomfield reach the sea; Black Mountain is black granite and Thornton Peak grey-topped with rainforest below; the mesh lies outside the map, never rises above the map's edge between its first-ring vertices, faces up, draws its farthest ring first and is drawn behind the world without depth. The old backdrop test went with the backdrop.

## v0.09: browser tests stop before changing range

**What failed.** After v0.08, two browser tests failed when they switched to low range: the keyboard driving test and the phone controls test. The phone test already failed on v0.07. Range only changes below 20 km/h (`Vehicle.toggleRange`), and both tests switched while the car could still be moving faster than that. The keyboard test held S for 3 s to stop, but S brakes and then reverses, and it left the car reversing at about 20 km/h. The phone test let go of the throttle at about 26 km/h and tapped HAND on and off, which doesn't wait for the car to stop. How fast the car was going at the switch depended on how long the screenshots took while the throttle was held (the software renderer is slower on the 6 km map), so the result turned on timing.

**Fix.** Both tests now stop the car on the park brake and wait until it is below 0.5 m/s before switching range. The game is unchanged.

## v0.10: an afternoon storm

**What the owner said.** The first level is "a bit rinse and repeat". Of five ideas the owner liked, this is the changing weather: a storm comes through in the afternoon, the clay gets slipperier and the creeks rise, so arriving late at a crossing is harder. (The other four, varied stretches, route choices, rewards for exploring and surprises, were worked on alongside this and are in their own sections.)

**The timeline.** `EpisodeDef.storm` (a `WeatherTimeline`, src/data/weatherTimeline.ts) sets how hard it rains at clock times, blended between; the CREB's is in src/data/episodes/crebWeather.ts so later episodes can have their own. The CREB starts as before, wet after overnight rain. Showers come in at 3 pm (0.6 by 3:20), build to heavy rain by 5 pm, ease from 5:45 and stop at 6:45. 3 pm was picked from the autopilot's clean run, which comes over Bob's Hill at 3:02, is on the concrete at 3:29, at Donovan's Creek at 3:57 and on the Bloomfield Track from 4:08 to 5:07; the owner's own faster pace (arriving around 4 pm) meets the rain on the Bloomfield Track.

**What the rain does.**

- **The ground** soaks up the rain, most of the way in 24 minutes (`soakHours` 0.4), and dries much more slowly (3 hours). Wetness goes from 0.7 (after rain) to 1 (the existing raining figure), so wet clay's grip falls from 0.58 to 0.43 and black soil's and dirt's with it. It is about 0.8 at 3:30 and over 0.95 by 5 pm.
- **The creeks** rise while it rains, at first 15 cm an hour in heavy rain, slowing as they near 50 cm, and fall back by 30% of the rise an hour once it eases; their current is 2% faster per centimetre of rise. Worked out minute by minute from the first point of the timeline, the rise is under 1 cm at 3:15, 7 cm at 4 pm, 15 cm at 5 pm, 22 cm at 5:45 and 24 cm at 6 pm, with the current up to 1.3–1.5 times its own. Every creek rises the same; the Daintree is crossed long before the rain.
- **The sky** goes from the after-rain overcast to the raining one as the rain grows (greyer, darker, weaker sun, nearer haze), the rain streaks fall in proportion, and the rain's hiss, patter and roof drumming follow it (they already followed `env.rain`).
- **The water** of a risen creek is drawn higher, its ripples and flecks run faster, and it turns muddy brown (55% at 30 cm up).
- **Notes.** The briefing has a new paragraph forecasting storms from 3 pm and naming the Little Annan as the crossing to reach early, and the welcome and loading screens add "storms from 3 pm" to the conditions. HUD notes show for 6 s at 3 pm ("Rain coming in — the clay will turn greasy and the creeks will rise"), 4:45 ("Heavy rain — the creeks are coming up fast") and 6 pm ("The rain is easing, but the creeks are still high").

**How the creeks rise.** The water grid only covers a creek's channel. Before the physics and meshes are built, a map with a storm spreads each channel's water level over the dry bank within 4 m of its edge that is less than the storm's highest rise above it (`spreadCreekBanks`, src/sim/world/flood.ts); that water lies under the ground until the creek rises, so nothing changes until then (a test checks the water depth at 4000 points is unchanged). Ground lower than the water beside a fall is left alone. The rise is then added to the water surface wherever it is creek water (within 4 m of a creek, `CREEK_MARGIN`), in the physics, the particles and the water mesh (the creek water is its own group, raised as a whole). Puddles and rain-filled mud holes always lie further off (8 m and 15 m), so they keep the level they were laid at, and so does the weather's own creek offset (`waterLevelOffset`, 5 cm after rain, which the puddles were laid for): the storm's rise is separate from it. The water mesh now tells puddles from creek by that 4 m margin (it was 2 m; no puddle lies closer than 5 m). New puddles don't appear while it rains, since they are pressed into the ground before the physics is built.

**Fair, and harder late.** Driven across the Little Annan with the autopilot from 22 m before the ford:

| Time (rise)        | Short-wheelbase 4WD crawling (low range, lockers) | Short-wheelbase 4WD at 16 km/h | Land Rover crawling |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------- |
| 3 pm (0)           | across, water 0.61 m                              | across                         | across              |
| 5 pm (15 cm)       | across, 0.76 m                                    | across                         | across              |
| 5:45 pm (22–23 cm) | swept away                                        | across, carried for 2.3 s      | across, 0.84 m      |

So a careful driver on schedule gets through in either vehicle, and a late one in the short-wheelbase 4WD has to keep its speed up through the ford or lose the car. At Donovan's Creek on schedule (4 pm, 7 cm) both vehicles get across in either range; an hour late (15 cm) the short-wheelbase 4WD in high range is pushed 60 m downstream, while low range with the lockers still crosses. Roaring Meg, crossed on schedule about 2:40 before the rain, already needs low range; a very late arrival in high range at 15 cm is carried towards the falls, as low range with the lockers still crosses.

**Camps and replays.** Everything follows from the clock alone, so restarting from a camp, which restores the clock, brings back the weather, the ground and the creeks as they were when you got there; the notes count from the restored hour, so one already passed doesn't show again. Replays with chosen conditions use the conditions' fixed weather and no storm.

**Time and fuel.** The autopilot's clean run in the storm (`scripts/creb-drive.ts`, which now follows the storm by default, or takes `clear`, `wet` or `rain` for fixed weather) takes 34.6 minutes of driving (34.3 before), arrives at 5:07 pm (5:04) with the creeks 16 cm up, and uses 55.7 L of petrol (54.5) in the short-wheelbase 4WDs and 35.6 L of diesel (35.1) in the Land Rover. Nothing was retuned.

**Debug.** The debug panel has a button that moves the clock on 30 minutes, to try the storm and the dusk without driving for them.

**Working alongside the other sessions.** This touches no obstacles, forks, pickups or triggered events. It adds new files (the timeline, the CREB's storm, the flood module) and only small changes to the episode definition, the environment blend, the simulation, the renderer, the water mesh, the particles and the game. A new obstacle or creek crossing from another piece rises with the rest of the creeks if it is within 4 m of a creek.

**Tests.** New `tests/storm.test.ts`: the timeline (dry before 3 pm, heavy by 5, easing at dusk), the clay's grip and the creeks' rise and current over the afternoon, the sky and rain following it, the spread banks changing no water depth until the creeks rise and leaving the mud holes alone; autopilot drives over the Little Annan at 3 pm and 5 pm and over Donovan's Creek at 4 pm in both vehicles, and at 5:45 pm the short-wheelbase 4WD crawling swept away but crossing at a steady 16 km/h, and the Land Rover crawling across in water over 15 cm deeper.
